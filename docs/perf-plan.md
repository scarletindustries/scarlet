# The speed plan

This is the build order for making the interpreter fast on one core, written on 23 November 2026. It follows from `docs/perf-diagnosis.md`. The five designs it orders are in `docs/perf-design-core.md`, `perf-design-heap.md`, `perf-design-arrays.md`, `perf-design-compiler.md` and `perf-design-text-and-maps.md`.

How it was made: for each area three designers each wrote a design from a different starting point, a judge merged them, three sceptics attacked the result, and the design was revised to answer them. One more pass put every step into the order below and found where the designs disagree.

Each step is built alone, measured with `benches/run.py`, and reverted if it does not pay. The owner took the recommended answer to decisions 1 to 10 below on 23 November 2026. 11 and 12 came up when step 10 was built and are open.

## What to expect

The start is 6.17 on instructions and 2.83 by the clock. These figures are my own combination of the five areas' numbers, with each gain reduced where an earlier step has already taken part of it. The gains overlap, so I did not multiply the areas' own totals: doing that gives about 1.4, which puts json, cfold and mandelbrot well under 1.0 and is not believable.

After the first third (steps 1 to 16: the array write, the value word, the heap, the interpreter core with today's ownership, and Update): about 3.1, and I would be surprised outside 2.8 to 3.5. The array write (6.17 to about 4.8) is firm, because the diagnosis measured it by ablation. The heap factor (about 0.84 here, against 0.826 measured on today's benchmarks) is the best-founded number in the plan, because it ran in a copy of the real VM. The core factor (about 0.75 to 0.80) is the soft one: three benchmarks were measured on hand-assembled programs and twenty are arithmetic, and no loader pass has been built.

After the second third (steps 17 to 35: the IR's shape, matching, Probe, the rest of the arrays, strings, binaries and maps): about 2.5, in a range of 2.2 to 2.9. Without the fannkuch source rewrite add about 0.08. The trie decision moves it by under 1%. The risk here is in single rows, not the geomean: matching is the riskiest step, and the string figures lean on probes whose free path is cheaper than the real one.

After the last third (steps 36 to 50: the middle end, ownership, then the gated steps): about 2.2, in a range of 1.9 to 2.6. If the owner stops before ownership it ends near 2.4. If every gated step (42 to 49) is declined it ends near 2.3, because together they are worth about 0.97. Step 50 is a cost of 1% to 2% and belongs to the processes work.

For comparison, the diagnosis estimated 2.3 to 2.5 for all twenty causes, CPython is 2.46 and Node with no JIT is 1.95. The plan lands a little better than the diagnosis because core's probe measured calls and Float instructions cheaper than the diagnosis assumed.

By the clock expect about half of this in logs, because most of what goes is cheap instructions: about 2.0 after the first third, 1.7 after the second and 1.5 at the end, each give or take 0.2. The array write and the string copies are real memory work, so those rows gain more by the clock than the call and dispatch rows do.

Where it will still be bad at the end: sieve (about 4 to 6), permute and queens (about 4 to 5), fannkuch (about 3 with its rewrite, about 9 without), and bounce, closures and richards (about 3 to 4). Rows that read below 1.0 by this arithmetic (json, cfold, mandelbrot) should not be trusted, and the diagnosis notes that LuaJIT's own number is inflated on spectral_norm, map_churn, cfold and closures. `benches/run.py --baseline`, added in step 1, is what will show which of these estimates was wrong, one step at a time: it prints how far each benchmark moved from a saved run, in machine instructions, VM instructions and cells made.

## Decisions

1 to 10 were each settled as the recommended answer. 11 and 12 are open.

### 1. docs/vm-design.md proposes that the VM counts references itself and that this is always right whatever Perceus did. Steps 39 to 41 end that: the compiler states every count, two checkers verify it, and the VM stops sweeping frames and testing registers on write. Do you want that?

- **A.** Yes. Build steps 39 and 40 with the VM still checking, and land step 41 only when every test and benchmark shows the VM's own counting released nothing. A compiler mistake after that is a leak or a typed stop, never undefined behaviour.
- **B.** No. Stop after step 38 and keep the net. fib stays near 165 instructions a call instead of 112, the geomean ends near 2.4 instead of 2.2, and borrowed parameters and Open from the compiler are not built.

Recommended: Option A. It is the last step for exactly this reason, it can be reverted alone, and the call, register and tail-call costs cannot be fixed soundly without it.

### 2. docs/vm-design.md records as Decided that an array stays the RRB tree. Step 23 replaces the tree above 32 elements with a strict trie. May it?

- **A.** Yes. One node shape and no size tables, a read at 5,000 elements costs 146 instead of 240, and a join costs about 44 instructions per element of the shorter side, so joining two long arrays gets slower (5 times at 5,000 a side, about 700 times at a million).
- **B.** No. Keep the RRB tree and build in-place push and uncons on it. The scoreboard ends within 1% of option A, and the merge, rebalance and size-table code stays.

Recommended: Option A, for simplicity and a worst case you can state, not for speed. No program in the repository joins two long arrays. Steps 1 to 22 do not depend on the answer.

### 3. What are the checked and the total array write called, and what does a write out of range do? Step 3 needs this before it starts.

- **A.** `array::set(xs, i, x)` returns `Result(Array(a), Nil)` and `array::put(xs, i, x)` returns the array, unchanged when `i` is outside it. Both write in place and cost the same.
- **B.** `array::set` is the total form (as Elm's is) and the checked form gets another name such as `array::try_set`.

Recommended: Option A. It follows the stdlib rule that a fallible operation returns Result, and the plain name is the one that tells you when it failed.

### 4. docs/vm-design.md records as Decided that there are no fused super-instructions. Core steps 8 and 9 add constant-operand forms, compare-and-jump forms and call forms split by whether an argument is shared, about 115 opcodes in all. May the loader choose such forms?

- **A.** Yes. Reword the line to say the loader picks a specific instruction from what it knows about the operands, as BEAM's loader does, and that there is still no peephole pass over emitted code.
- **B.** No. Keep one instruction per Core construct. mandelbrot keeps about 160 of the 508 instructions a turn that step 8 would remove, the bare loop is about 130 a step instead of 87, and fib pays 3 to 23 more a call.

Recommended: Option A. Each form is one Core construct with a fact the loader already holds, and a JIT maps each to a template more easily, not less.

### 5. The 256-register window sets limits a program can meet: 250 parameters to a function, 254 values live at once in one function, 65,520 constructors with fields, 16 GiB of heap per process, and small Ints of plus or minus 2^50. A function over 254 registers stops when it is called until step 48 is built. Is that acceptable?

- **A.** Yes. Over 250 parameters is a compile error, and a function too wide for the window stops with NotBuiltYet until step 48. The widest of the 1,352 functions in the repository needs about 133.
- **B.** No. Build the spill path (step 48) straight after step 10, before anything else depends on the window, so no valid program can stop.

Recommended: Option A, and move step 48 up the day a real program hits it. The spill path is a second storage class that only its own tests would exercise today.

### 6. The inliner (steps 37 and 38) can copy a function from one module into another. That ties the caller's code to the callee's, which works against replacing one module while a program runs. Where may it inline?

- **A.** Anywhere in the program. `same_unit` says yes to every pair of modules, and can be narrowed later.
- **B.** Only inside one module, and from the stdlib into any module. A call into another user module stays a call.

Recommended: Option B from the start. Every measured gain is either inside one module (bounce, spectral_norm, towers, map_churn) or a stdlib function inlined into user code (json's `result::then`, the folds in closures), so nothing on the scoreboard is lost and a module's code depends on no other user module.

### 7. Step 26 feeds a string's whole words to the hash, so `==` and the hash read the same words. Every map with string keys changes its order once, and golden outputs that print one change with it. Accept that?

- **A.** Yes. One hash path, and a string-key lookup falls from about 1,664 instructions to about 600.
- **B.** No. Keep today's hash values by feeding the last partial word as bytes, which keeps a second path in the hasher.

Recommended: Option A, with a changeset. The project is pre-release and the order of a map was never promised.

### 8. Text's kernels read a string's words as bytes with `bytemuck::cast_slice` and search with `memchr`. bytemuck is a new dependency with `unsafe` inside it, as memchr, smallvec and simd-json already have. The workspace itself stays free of `unsafe`. Add it?

- **A.** Yes. Add bytemuck and a compile error on a big-endian target. All four release targets are little-endian.
- **B.** No new dependency. Compare and hash by words and search with hand-written word loops, which loses memchr and memmem on split, contains and replace.

Recommended: Option A. It is one safe function over plain integers, and the alternative is slower code that is harder to read.

### 9. Several steps add public stdlib functions: `array::set`, `array::put`, `array::swap` and `array::repeat`, and `string::join` and `string::concat`. `string::replace` and `int::from_string` become built-ins with the same signatures. Add the new names?

- **A.** Yes. `string::join` is the documented linear way to build a long string, and `array::repeat` is the way to make a filled array.
- **B.** Add only the array write of step 3 now and keep the rest private until someone asks.

Recommended: Option A. Appending in place cannot be seen in the source, so the stdlib needs one spelling that is linear whatever else holds the string.

### 10. Steps 3 and 24 change benchmark sources: sieve, permute, queens and fannkuch call `array::put`, and later sieve builds its flags with `array::repeat` and fannkuch reverses in place. The scoreboard then measures different Scarlet programs. Do both?

- **A.** Yes to both. I checked benches/fannkuch/fannkuch.lua: the Lua twin already swaps in place, and the Erlang twins already use array:set, so this makes the programs match their twins.
- **B.** Only the step 3 change. fannkuch then ends near 11 times LuaJIT instead of about 4.

Recommended: Option A, with each row's old and new source measured on the same binary on the day it changes, so the source change and the VM change are never read as one number.

### 11. Open. Step 10 gives a stack a limit that question 5 does not name: 32 GiB, because where a frame starts is a 32-bit number. Past it a run stops with "the program ran out of stack". Is that acceptable?

- **A.** Yes. It is 1,073 million levels of the plainest recursion, which is past what a machine with 32 GiB of memory can hold anyway.
- **B.** No. Make the number 64 bits and the test for room one that checks for overflow. The stack's limit is then the machine's memory.

Recommended: Option A. The 32-bit number is part of what took the board from 0.901 to 0.856 at step 10, and B's cost was not measured by itself.

### 12. Open. A run that stops says what went wrong and not where. Step 10 has what it takes to say more: the place in the code, and the links down the stack. Should a stopped run print the functions it was in?

- **A.** No. What a stopped program says stays as it is, and `fail`, the cold function every failing arm would return through, is never built.
- **B.** Yes, on standard error after the message. `fail` stores the place, and the trace is a walk down the links.

Recommended: Option B, at step 40, which writes the call-site maps that a trace reads its callers from. Step 40 builds whichever is chosen.

## Where the designs disagreed, and how each is settled

- The value word. Core moves every tag (small Int is the 13 top ones, so 0xFFF8 and up; immediates 0x7FF8, function 0x7FF9, cell 0x7FFC, nullary 0x7FFD). Heap still writes references as 0xFFFC and nullary as 0xFFFD, and builds JumpUnlessTag constants from them. Text takes 0xFFFE for a frozen literal, and heap also reserves 0xFFFE for a shared constant arena. Under core's layout 0xFFFC to 0xFFFE are all small Ints. Settle it in step 5 with one table in value.rs that every area uses: core's five tags, plus 0x7FFE for a frozen literal, plus the empty array as immediate 0x7FF8 | 3. Heap's reference becomes 0x7FFC | variant (bits 32 to 47) | index (bits 0 to 30), and its tag-test constants become 0x7FFC_0000 | k and 0x7FFD_0000 | k. Text's map position gets 50 bits of small Int instead of 47, which only helps.
- String and binary literals have two designs. Heap step 2 makes each literal once per process as a cell with a frozen count and remembers it in `consts`. Text step 1 keeps literals out of the heap in a frozen area of the loaded program, behind a value tag, and measured that at 26 instructions against 57 for a sticky cell. Take text's design for String and Binary constants, because it is measured cheaper, costs nothing per process and is what docs/vm-design.md already proposes. Heap step 2 then freezes only big Int constants, and its 10.7% of string_keys is booked to text step 1. Core's side tables for strings and binaries are not needed either: a literal is an immediate word inside `Const`. The frozen area's cells must use heap step 3's header, so text step 1 comes after it.
- The cell header against the array cells. Heap's header says the first `scan` words after the header are values, with 255 as the escape for kinds that must be asked. Arrays put a raw window word at word 1 of the flat cell and three raw words at the front of the root, which would force the escape on the cells the array benchmarks free most. Settle it when arrays step 6 is built: put values first in both cells (flat: header, slots, then the window word last, scan = capacity; root: header, head, tree, tail, then the three raw words, scan = 3). An ArrayNode is a header and 32 values, so scan = 32. Only the three map kinds keep the escape. Step 11 built the escape as a scan of 0 and not 255, so that the one compare a field read makes (`i < scan`) does not pass for a map's or an array's cell. Arrays' masked compare (kind is ArrayFlat and count is 1) works on the new header unchanged.
- Raw cell sizes against the string rules. Heap step 2 rounds raw cells up to size classes. Text's invariant S3 says a string cell has exactly ceil(len / 8) data words, and its in-place append relies on the only room being the zero bytes of the last word. Settle it by leaving rounding out of step 7 and landing it in step 27 with `text::write`, the one maker of strings. From then on the rule is text's later one: capacity is the cell's size, the live words are ceil(len / 8), the last live word is zero padded, and spare words may hold anything. Step 30's append then writes into spare words as well, and step 46 only adds reallocation. A string past 253 words lives in a box in heap's `outside` table, so text, binary and map code must ask the heap for `data(cell)` (the words after the header) and never for one slice from the header on. Text step 11's request to raise DENSE_SIZES is moot after step 7.
- Noticing a dead cell. Core step 6a and compiler step 0 want `release` and `share` to return a typed Broken when the count is 0. Heap step 2 makes dup and drop infallible and sets a `stale` field that is read later. Settle it as a typed result from the out-of-line branch only. Heap's inline paths already test the count range (drop: above 1; dup: above 0) and call out of line otherwise, so that out-of-line function returns Err(DeadCell) for a count of 0 and the hot path gains nothing. The stop then happens at the instruction, which is what vm-design.md asks for, and `stale` is not needed. The exact live count that step 1 adds is replaced in step 7 by heap's audit walk, run by the test harness at exit.
- Three audits. Heap has `Heap::audit(roots)`, core has a test run that recounts owners after every instruction, and compiler has a quarantine of freed cells, read checks and a poison word in debug builds. Build one: heap's audit, with core's verifier maps and the globals as its roots, run after every instruction in a test mode. The poison word is the cell tag with index 0, which heap guarantees is never a cell. Quarantine must be a switch and not every debug build, because the allocation-count tests assert that a freed cell is taken again.
- One instruction enum. Heap, arrays, compiler and text all describe new instructions in today's `Instr` style, with boxed operand lists and a `moved` bool each, and four of them separately change `Instr::writes` (arrays for ArraySplit, heap for Unpack, text for MapNext, core through `Ins::operands`). Core step 3 replaces the enum. Settle it by landing core steps 3 and 4 (steps 9 and 10) before any other area adds an instruction, apart from the small ArraySet and Move of steps 2 and 3. Every later instruction is written once as `Ins<R>` with u8 registers, the window's look, take and put, and inline operands or a `lists` entry with one bit per entry (core's copy bit, text's `show`). I checked the sizes: every proposed instruction fits 16 bytes, and a constructor form with its shape inline has room for seven fields, so heap's wish for fixed forms up to five fields is met. `Ins::operands` must carry two things core did not list: several written registers, with a clash edge between every pair (arrays' rule, which Unpack and MapNext also follow), and a write that happens only on the hit edge of an instruction with a miss target.
- Who decides ownership. Arrays and text set `moved` and `own_first` in the loader's `moves_on_last_use`. Heap puts `moved` on Con and Update and has the loader ignore reuse. Core wants Perceus to state move or copy and the loader only to encode. Compiler writes Lend, Give and Copy on every operand and deletes `moves_on_last_use`. These are one fact stated four ways. Settle it in two stages. Until step 39 the loader's liveness is the one place every such flag is set, which is text's S0 with every register owning. At steps 39 and 40 compiler's marks become the only statement, and the loader encodes them as core's `dups` bits, kill bits and `Drop`. `own_first` is then a Give on a keeping operand, so text's S0 becomes checker rule V4. Compiler's `sig.rs` is the one table of operand positions and core's `Ins::operands` is written from it. Concat's first part, ArraySet, ArrayPush, ArraySlice and ArrayJoin sources, MapSet's map and Update's base must be added to its keeping list, operand by operand.
- Three classes or two. Core's contract has scalar, int and any, with a never-a-cell bit per RTy in ResolvedPool. Compiler has `Repr { Word, Counted }` from `Program::repr`, with Int as Counted, and lets a Give sit on any looking operand. Settle it with compiler's two values in the IR and one accessor, `Program::repr`. Its data must be filled by the compiler for every type a body mentions, not only the types a program builds or matches, which is core's point. The loader reads 'this local is an Int' from the bind's type and uses it for core's `Int` verifier state and kill bits. A Give on a looking operand becomes a kill bit where the instruction has one and a `Drop` after it otherwise. Compiler's 'Ret releases what its folded Drop names' and core's 'Ret releases nothing' are settled as core's: the Drop stays its own instruction before Ret, and a multi-register Drop makes that cheap.
- Two verifiers. Compiler checks Counted Core in scarlet_ir and returns `Verified`. Core checks the emitted `Ins<Reg>` in the VM and returns `Checked`. Keep both, because each checks a different crate's work: the first proves scarlet_lower balanced every count, and the second proves the loader's translation and register allocation kept that (a register shared by two live owned values, a wrong shift, a write to register 0) and writes the call-site maps. `load` takes `Verified` and `Machine::new` takes `Checked`. They share one test: core's generated test per instruction is compiler's table test of every handler against its `Sig` row. Watch the `startup` row for the cost of two walks.
- Reuse tokens. Core keeps DropReuse, a `Token` verifier state, `fits_ctor` and an `entry:` and `loop:` label per function for tokens carried round a loop. Heap retires tokens in its step 5 and measured that on the real VM as a gain on every benchmark that uses them (cfold 7.9% fewer instructions). Compiler retires them at its step 9 and expected cfold to lose up to 4%. Settle it with heap's measurement: tokens go at step 13, before core's tail-call step. Core then needs no Token state, no `fits_ctor` and one entry label, and compiler's predicted cfold loss does not happen because the heap of step 7 pops the freed cell straight back.
- Unpack and Open are one instruction. Heap's Unpack is found by a loader pattern on a `Match` arm that starts with the scrutinee's Drop. Compiler's Open is placed by the rc pass, and compiler step 4 deletes `Match`, which the loader pattern needs. Settle it with one VM instruction and one name, `Open`. Step 13 lands it with heap's loader pattern, which is what was measured. Step 18 must carry it over when `Match` goes: the old Perceus emits `CoreExpr::Open` at a switch arm where the scrutinee is dead on entry, and the loader pattern is deleted, or list, binary_trees and cfold lose step 13's gain. Step 42 only widens where Open is placed, so most of compiler step 11's 0.992 is already booked at step 13.
- Record update has three designs on the same code. Arrays step 2 changes `construct` in translate.rs to read kept fields first. Compiler step 2 replaces those reads with `Atom::Update`. Heap step 6 adds `Update` and `Take` with reuse specialisation in Perceus and a hole check in the loader. Compiler step 13 turns an update that reads its own base into Open and a fresh constructor, and measured that turning every rebuild into Update made permute worse. Settle it like this. `Atom::Update` is one node, built once at step 16, with heap's VM half (operands first, shape compare, copy path) and compiler's lowering rule (only from a source `..base` whose variant is known). The lowering emits Update when no given argument reads the base, and keeps arrays' kept-fields-first order with a fresh constructor when one does, because then the base must die before the argument is computed. That is compiler step 13's result reached in the lowering, so step 44 has little left to do. `Take`, holes and reuse specialisation of ordinary rebuilds are a gated step (45): they are the cheapest form on the toy (157 against 311) but add a state nothing at run time can check.
- Find, Probe and Find. Arrays step 3 defines `Find` with one bind and four ops. Compiler step 5 defines `Probe` with several binds and four other ops. Text step 13 defines `Find` with several binds and nine ops, lowered a third way. Build one node at step 19: compiler's shape (several binds, a hit arm and a miss arm) with the union of the ops. The miss rule must be arrays': a miss takes nothing and writes nothing, because `array::put` returns the same array on a miss and the next pattern arm still reads it. Compiler's 'a kept operand is consumed on both paths' is dropped, and the checker applies a probe's gives on entry to the hit arm only. The box is avoided in two ways that do not overlap: the decision tree emits a bare probe when a match is directly on the call (compiler), and the simplifier's jump-with-a-known-constructor rule removes the rest after inlining (text's general rule, which is compiler step 6 rule 6). Text step 9's widened MapGet pattern is not built, because step 19 comes first and deletes `option_arms`.
- Array patterns. Arrays step 4 adds `CorePat::Array`. Compiler step 4 deletes `CorePat` and `Match`. Settle it by order: matching lands first (step 18), and array patterns are then checks in the decision tree (step 20), which the arrays design already allows for. The read-then-commit rule becomes a non-consuming head probe, the nested tests and guard, then `ArrayDrop`. The VM half of arrays step 4 (ArrayUncons, ArraySplit, ArrayPush) is unchanged.
- Variant tests. Heap step 3 gives `JumpUnlessTag` on the top 32 bits of the value. Compiler step 4 gives `Switch` and estimated its cost on a toy that reads the tag from memory. They fit together if heap goes first: Switch is a table jump on bits 32 to 47 of the value word with no load, and JumpUnlessTag stays as the one-way and two-way form. Both need `Program::types` to list every variant a program names with its field count, so that change lands once, at step 11.
- The closure a frame runs as. Core puts it in register 1 and reads captures through it. Heap says GetCapture stays its own instruction because the closure is not in a register. Compiler makes it `params[0]` and a capture an ordinary part read. After step 10 the closure is in a register, so settle it as compiler's IR (env is `params[0]`), pinned by the loader to register 1, with a capture read as a field read at index + 1 of the closure cell. Heap's closure layout (header, function word, captures) is what core's CallValue reads.
- The scratch Vec. Core step 5 deletes `passing`, heap step 4 deletes `Machine::args` but leaves `Instr::Array` using `passing`, and arrays steps 4 and 6 make array literals fill their cell directly. In this order core step 5 (step 14) lands before arrays, so `Instr::Array` keeps a small buffer of its own until step 22.
- The tail-argument scheduling pass (core step 5) is written into scarlet_lower before compiler's passes exist. It must run last before count insertion, after inlining and specialisation, and step 17 ports it from `Tail(Call)` to `TailCall`. The inliner also removes calls core made cheap, so core's and compiler's gains on closures, spectral_norm, map_churn and towers must be read from the scoreboard and not added.
- Gains booked by more than one area. Each of these counts once, in the step that lands first. Literals: heap 2 and text 1 (text). Ownership: core 6 and compiler 9 and 10 (core's table). Open: heap 5 and compiler 11 (step 13). Update: heap 6, compiler 2 and diagnosis cause 12 (step 16). An array or map held in a record being unique at its write: arrays 2, compiler 13, heap's Take and text 10 and 11 for richards (step 4 measured 0 on richards: 7.2% of it is booked at step 33 and the rest at step 44). The Some round trip on a map lookup: text 9 and arrays 3 (step 19). The `''` default in string_keys: arrays 3 and text 1. Dead field reads and failed tests: heap 3 and 5 and compiler 4. Tuples in bounce and closures in json: heap 4 and compiler 6 and 7. map and filter in closures: arrays 8 and compiler 7 and 8. The cell work inside array writes: heap 1 and 2 measured it on today's benchmarks, and step 3 deletes most of it first.

## The order

### Step 1. Instruments and a heap that notices a dead cell. `scarlet dis --instrs` prints the loaded instructions, a per-opcode run counter is added, benches/run.py prints cycles beside instructions and, with `--baseline`, how far each benchmark moved from a saved run, the live-cell count is exact, the golden harness asserts no cell is live at exit, and `release` and the unique test return a typed `Broken::DeadCell` on a freed cell.

From: compiler 0, core 6a, core 1 (scoreboard only).

**Why here.** Every later step is judged on these numbers, and every later step that touches counts relies on a doubled release being loud. It may also turn up a bug that exists today, which is better found before anything changes.

**Expected gain.** None. The scoreboard must not move, and that is the acceptance test.

**What happened.** The board moved. Every benchmark but sieve got cheaper, by 1.7% on the geomean (6.17 to 6.06), and sieve read +0.1%, which is inside its own run-to-run spread. None of the gain is the step's work. The compiler laid `Machine::call` out differently, and each VM instruction lost about 3.5 machine instructions: one turn of a bare loop went from 346 to 332. The step's real cost sits under that. It is about one instruction for each release call, two for each cell freed, and a few on each owned array or map edit, and it is why sieve, which runs the fewest VM instructions, shows no gain. The next edit to `Machine::call` can lose the 1.7% again for no better reason.

So until step 9 replaces the loop, the board's net change does not judge a step that claims under about 2%. Such a step is judged by the exact counts from `scarlet run --counters` (VM instructions, cells made and cells reused), and by probes: small loops that each run one path, measured as the slope between two sizes, with the change in a bare loop taken off. The probes are in the perf scratch directory under `step1/answer`, with the script that counts one loop turn by function under lldb and the ones that compare call sites and function sizes between two builds.

### Step 2. `Move` takes its source at a last use: `Instr::Move { dst, src, moved }`, set by `moves_on_last_use`, with the test that two spellings of the same function make the same number of cells.

From: arrays 0 (and the Move half of text 6). Needs: 1.

**Why here.** It makes 'the count is 1' mean what the array write, the string append and the map edit all need, and it is a few lines on today's loader.

**Expected gain.** No scoreboard claim. It removes a share and a release per joined heap value (about 70 instructions each).

**What happened.** The VM's own counts did not move on any benchmark. The same instructions ran and the same cells were made and reused, because no benchmark has the shape the test pins. In that test a branch that ends in a name made 4 cells and now makes 3, the same as the branch that ends in the call. On the board the geomean moved by 0.06%. The rows whose Moves carry cells got cheaper: fannkuch by 0.73%, sieve by 0.47%, json by 0.10%, richards by 0.06% and strings by 0.05%. Every other row is inside 0.05% either way, which is what the rows that run no Move at all show too.

Probes give the cost of one Move exactly, as the slope between two sizes with a bare loop beside them, which did not move. A Move that takes a cell is 74 machine instructions cheaper than one that shares it and leaves the old register for a later `Drop` to release. A Move of a cell that is read again is 3 dearer, which is the test of the flag. A Move of an Int or a Bool is 1 dearer. The handler looks at the value before the flag to get that last number: only a cell has a reference to hand over, so any other value is copied and its register left alone. The first version emptied the register whatever it held, which cost 4 on every Int and Bool, and bounce, permute and queens read 0.05% to 0.13% worse with it.

Not built here: a Move the register allocator gave one register for both ends (`r3 = Move move r3`) does nothing, and every Move that permute, queens, map_churn and string_keys run is one, 1.8 to 4.2 million times each. Step 9's loader should not emit them. The owning-register set that text's step 6 passes to `moves_on_last_use` is not built either. Every register owns its reference today, so the set would be all of them, and it belongs with the borrowing that first makes it smaller.

### Step 3. A write at an index on today's tree: the `ArraySet` instruction with a miss edge, `array::set`, `array::put` and `array::swap`, and the four array benchmarks changed to call `array::put`.

From: arrays 1. Needs: 2, the owner's answer on the two names.

**Why here.** It is the largest single gain in the whole effort, it is low risk, and it needs nothing else. It goes first so that every later measurement is taken on benchmarks that no longer spend most of their time rebuilding arrays.

**Expected gain.** Arrays' arithmetic gives a geomean factor of 0.788, so 6.17 becomes about 4.86 (sieve 328 to about 14, permute 32 to about 12, queens 30 to about 10, fannkuch 28 to about 21). The diagnosis priced it at 0.776. The copy path's cost (about 1,400) is an estimate.

**What happened.** The board moved as forecast. The geomean went from 6.05 to 4.79, a factor of 0.792 against the 0.788 expected. Three runs of the board gave the factor as 0.792 to 0.793, and the two ends moved by 0.01 with LuaJIT's own rows. sieve went from 325 times LuaJIT to 13.9 (about 14 expected), permute from 32.1 to 12.9 (12.4), queens from 29.2 to 10.5 (10.1) and fannkuch from 27.2 to 20.8 (20.9). The other 19 rows did not move. The whole gain is the four sources calling `array::put`. On the old sources the new binary runs the same VM instructions and makes and reuses the same cells as the old one on all 23 rows, and its machine instructions are within 0.13% on every row in every run. strings reads 0.06% to 0.07% worse each time, which is far under what the board can judge.

The exact counts, old source to new on the new binary: sieve ran 20,668,414 VM instructions and now runs 8,713,896, and made 9,858,420 cells and now makes 469,620. permute went from 164,199,409 to 83,146,211 and from 19,019,201 cells to 6,771,401. queens went from 253,568,006 to 92,736,008 and from 20,877,500 to 8,848,000. fannkuch went from 108,819,434 to 105,285,484 and from 20,483,142 to 15,299,877. They run 498,105, 4,031,200, 1,582,000 and 986,408 writes. Not all of those are in place. A review run that forced one write site at a time to copy found that every write in sieve and fannkuch is in place, that half of permute's writes copy (2,015,600 of them, which step 4 should end), and that every write in queens copies, because `place_queen` keeps the board to backtrack to. So queens' gain is a copying write in place of a rebuild, and queens will keep copying.

The write costs more than the arrays design booked, and sieve landing on its estimate does not show otherwise. Probes give an in-place write about 390 machine instructions on an array of 32 elements or fewer and about 620 at 5,000, against the design's 170 and 367. Most of it is cell lookups: two reads of the root, then a unique test and a node read at each level. The design counted sieve as 498,105 writes at 367 on top of a remainder of 1,047M. Measured, the writes are about 311M and the remainder about 930M, and the two errors cancel. Forecasts for sieve at steps 6, 22 and 23 should start from the measured write. A copying write costs 1,652 at 6 elements, 2,101 at 16, 6,938 at 1,000 and 7,964 at 5,000, against the estimated 1,400, which is why permute and queens sit 4% above their estimates. A write that misses costs about 190. A loop turn that misses costs 295 more than a bare turn, because the `Const`, the `Move` on the miss edge and the `Drop` run too.

One walk was built, not the design's two. The design has an in-place function beside a general copying one, because its general path measured 2 to 2.6 times slower on an array nothing else holds. `set` in array.rs walks once from the root. It uses a node as it is when the node's count is 1, and otherwise copies it and puts the copy in the node above. On an array nothing else holds, that makes the same lookups as the in-place function would.

The checked `array::set` does not yet cost what `put` costs, though decision 3 says it does. The loader builds `Ok` on the hit edge and `Err(Nil)` on the miss edge, and the two join before the program's `match`. So an `Err` arm that gives the array back, or `array::set(xs, i, x) or xs`, is reached from the hit edge as far as liveness can tell, and the write shares the array and copies the path. At 16 elements that write costs 2,874 and makes 3 cells. It costs 1,117 and makes 1 cell (the `Ok`) when the `Err` arm does not read the array, and `put` costs 468 and makes none. Filling 5,000 elements that way makes 24,984 cells. The test `a_checked_write_copies_when_its_err_arm_reads_the_array` pins that number, and step 19 should bring it to 5,000 or fewer. The loader's `MapGet` recognition is no short cut to that: it needs the scrutinee to have no other read, and Perceus drops a `Result` in both arms, so the loader would have to delete drops it did not write. Until step 19, the stdlib comment, docs/semantics.md and the changeset say where the copy happens and point at `put`.

Some things are left for the steps that own them. `set` in array.rs reads the array's length again after the handler has tested it. For an index it does not have, or a size table that hides the slot, it gives the value up and returns the array as if it had written. The two reads cannot disagree today. Step 21 should have one function find the slot and hand `set` a witness of it, with a typed fault for a broken tree, and the second read (25 to 30 instructions) goes with it. A write to a range builds the whole range as a tree first. A loop that keeps `0..5000` and writes to it pays about 316,000 instructions and 164 cells a write, and one that does not keep it builds it once. The miss code for `put` is inline, so every hit runs a `Jump` (18), and `array::swap` builds two `Some` a call. Step 19 removes both. fannkuch's `swap01` and `swap12` write a 0 into an array shorter than their indices, where the old arms left it alone, and they should call `array::swap` once it costs no more. `ArraySet` and `MapFind` now say in `Instr::writes` that they write on the hit edge only (`Write::OnHit`), and liveness reads the miss edge from that value. It is the fact step 9's `Ins::operands` carries for every instruction with a miss edge.

### Step 4. A record update reads its kept fields straight after the base is evaluated (`construct` in translate.rs), so the base dies before the new field is computed.

From: arrays 2. Needs: 3.

**Why here.** It is a small lowering change with a measured saving, and it is the rule step 16 keeps for an update whose new value reads the base.

**Expected gain.** About 0.990 (permute about 12.4 to about 9.8), measured at 2,108 instructions a step on a hand-edited source. Measure richards here too: its `map::set(s.tasks, ..)` is the same shape, and if its map becomes unique this step takes the 10% that three later steps also claim.

**What happened.** permute got about two thirds of its forecast, and richards got nothing yet. The geomean went from 4.80 to 4.77, a factor of 0.9934 against the 0.990 expected, and three more runs of the board gave 0.9935 to 0.9936. permute went from 12.89 times LuaJIT to 11.04, where 9.8 was expected, and runs 14.31% fewer machine instructions. The VM runs the same instructions as before on all 23 rows, kind for kind, because the step only changes the order of the field reads. Cells made and reused are the same on 22 rows. permute made 6,771,401 cells and now makes 3,748,201, and reuses 2,016,000 as before. Of the other rows only richards (0.22% to 0.25% worse) and nbody (0.10% worse) moved by more than 0.05% on a quiet machine. Rows that load the same instructions from both binaries show what the board cannot judge: bounce and closures read 0.03% better on two runs and level on two, and on a run that shared the machine with a build, fib and queens read 0.19% and 0.18% worse.

permute still copies at 504,000 of its writes, where it copied at 2,015,600. Each copy is two cells, and each one avoided saved 1,164 machine instructions. The ones left are not this step's to end. `swaps` and `permute` build a `State`, pass it to a call, and keep its cell so that a later `State` can take it, and a cell kept that way still holds the array while the call runs. So the array has two holders in the callee, and the first write under each `permute(_, 1)` copies it, 2,520 times a run (and 360 times with five elements, which is how many such calls there are). With reuse switched off in a test build, permute makes 400 array cells, which are its 200 literals, no write copies, and it runs at 10.38 times LuaJIT. Step 13 takes reuse tokens away, and those copies go with them. That leaves permute near 10.4 and not 9.8, because a write costs more than the arrays design counted (step 3).

richards did not move, and its map is still copied at its sets, 2,476,025 of them. The step does its part: in `put_task`, `release` and `create_task` the record is now given up before the set. Two other things stand in the way. The loader only lets a set take its map when the map's drop is the first thing after the set, and Perceus orders drops by local number, so in `put_task` the task's drop comes first and the set is loaded as the plain built-in, which copies. 2,243,600 of the sets load that way. And `hold_self` and `queue_packet` read `s.hold_count` and `s.queue_count` in an argument written after the set, so the record has to live past it. Test builds give the sizes. A loader that finds the map's drop behind other drops makes 20,586,083 cells where 23,910,908 are made now, which is about two thirds of the sets in place, and richards runs 7.2% fewer machine instructions than it did before this step. With those two reads also moved before the update in the source, it makes 18,958,933 cells and runs 10.8% fewer, and all but about 40 sets are in place. The same loader change on the old field order saves no cell at all, so this step is needed for that 10% but does not deliver it. Step 33 sets the flag from liveness and books the 7.2%. The rest needs the two reads moved, by step 44 or in the benchmark's source.

Reading early has three costs, and all are measured. The first is register width. A kept field now lives in a register while the new fields are computed, so some functions need a register more and some one fewer. A register costs about 8 machine instructions each time a frame is swept, and 21 in a frame that keeps cells for reuse. That is all of nbody's 0.10%: with every frame padded to the same size the two orders run the same count. json has one lambda a register wider and read 0.02% worse on three runs and 0.02% better on the fourth, which is under what the board can judge. The second cost is richards' 0.22% to 0.25%, and it stays when the frames are padded. When the write still copies, the record now dies before the old map does, so the old map is freed by a second pass of `release` and not inside the record's. The four runs of the board put it at 77.0M to 86.3M machine instructions over the 2,243,600 sets that load as the plain built-in, 34 to 39 an update. A count of two scheduler cycles under lldb gave 28, which is too low. That cost goes when the set is in place. The third cost is a cell that is no longer reused, in one shape no benchmark has. When the base was built in the same function, its drop leaves its cell for the new record to take, and that drop used to sit straight before the new constructor. It now sits before the fields written after the base. If one of those is an `if`, `match`, `&&` or `||` used as a value, the reuse walk in perceus.rs forgets every waiting cell at the join, so the old cell is freed and the new record is made fresh. `t = P(i, i, i)` and then `P(..t, c: if i > 5 { 1 } else { 2 })` made 10,001 cells in 10,000 turns and reused 10,000, and now makes 20,001 and reuses none. A field that is a call or plain arithmetic keeps the cell. Step 13 takes reuse away for every shape, and step 16 updates this one where it is, since no field it is given reads the base.

One thing was built that the step did not ask for. A base with nothing to run counts as written first, so `State(v: f(s.v), ..s)` lowers to the same code as `State(..s, v: f(s.v))`. That is a name, or a field or tuple element read off a name, so the nested `Outer(inner: Inner(v: f(o.inner.v), ..o.inner), ..o)` writes in place too: 5,000 such updates made 29,984 cells and now make the 10,000 records. `runs_nothing` in translate.rs decides it, with one arm for each kind of expression. A base that has to run keeps its place among the arguments.

One thing a program can see does change, which the arrays design missed when it said a field read has no effect. The read has none, but the earlier drop it allows has one when the old record held the last reference to a handle. In `Held(..h, conn: redial(h.port))` the connection `h` held was open until the new record was built, and is now closed before `redial` runs. That is the rule every other value already follows, given up at its last use, so it stays, and docs/semantics.md and the changeset say so. The changeset is `major` for that reason. The order in which the base and the given fields run does not change: the program in `a_record_update_runs_its_parts_in_the_order_written` prints the same lines on the old binary and the new one.

The tests in crates/scarlet_vm/tests/run.rs pin the counts. In `a_record_update_gives_up_the_record_before_it_runs_the_new_fields`, filling 5,000 elements of an array held in a record made 24,984 cells and now makes 5,000, the records themselves. It also pins the spellings that still copy. An argument after the write that reads the base is the order the source asked for. An update inside an `if` or `match` used as a value copies because Perceus puts no drop inside a join, which the compiler design ends at step 17, where Perceus walks into a join. And a record kept for reuse across the call copies each node of the tree once, 164 cells, which step 13 ends. `a_nested_record_update_writes_in_place_wherever_its_spread_is` pins the nested update at 10,000. `a_record_update_sets_a_map_it_holds_in_place` pins a map set at 3,000 cells for 1,000 sets where it made 5,436, and pins the `put_task` shape at 5,436 for step 33 to bring down. `a_record_update_loses_its_cell_to_a_branch_in_a_later_field` pins the third cost. Two tests with a listener as the peer pin when a connection only the old record held is closed.

The binary, the boards, the probes and the test builds are in the perf scratch directory under `step4`, and what the reviews of the step added is under `step4/answer`.

### Step 5. The final value word: small Ints of 51 bits under the 13 top ones, immediates, function, cell and nullary at 0x7FF8, 0x7FF9, 0x7FFC and 0x7FFD, with 0x7FFE reserved for a frozen literal and immediate 3 for the empty array. `Value::finite` and `Value::float` become the only ways in from an f64, with the test that every tagged word is a NaN.

From: core 1. Needs: 1.

**Why here.** It is a format every other area builds on (heap's variant in the reference, text's frozen tag, arrays' empty immediate), it is low risk, and it must be settled once before any of them is written.

**Expected gain.** 0.989: map_churn loses its big-Int excursion (22.2%, measured by the diagnosis).

**What happened.** map_churn moved as forecast, and the rest of the board moved too, for a reason that has to be read apart from it. The geomean went from 4.77 to 4.66, a factor of 0.977 against the 0.989 expected (0.9770 to 0.9771 on four runs of the board). map_churn went from 2.49 times LuaJIT to 1.91 and runs 23.3% fewer machine instructions (19,428M to 14,909M), where the diagnosis measured 22.2%. Alone that is a factor of 0.9886. The other 22 rows are 1.2% cheaper between them, which the step did not claim: mandelbrot 3.7%, spectral_norm 2.8%, deriv 2.1%, list 1.9%, nbody 1.8%, tak 1.5%, and the rest between 0.1% (storage) and 1.4%. No row got dearer. LuaJIT's own list row has two readings, 6,210M and 6,640M, and three of this step's five boards got the dearer one. On those the geomean reads 4.75 to 4.64. Step 4 ended at 4.77 on the cheaper reading, so 4.77 to 4.66 is the pair to carry forward, and step 6's forecast starts from 4.66. The factor does not depend on LuaJIT's rows.

The VM's counts say what changed in the programs. Every benchmark runs the same VM instructions as before, kind for kind, and reuses the same cells. Cells made moved on three rows. map_churn made 9,862,974 and now makes 821,672. The 9,041,302 that went are the key generator's: `x * 69069` is past 2^47 on about half its calls, and both the product and the `+ 1` after it were heap cells, each costing 500 machine instructions to make and free. strings makes 68,366 fewer (17,794,789 to 17,726,423): on that many of its 120,000 rows the number `int::from_string` reads off the row's digits is between 2^47 and 2^50. nbody makes 2 fewer, the two energies it prints, which are near 1.7 times 10^14. The unchanged VM instructions are a fact about the board and not about the step. The loader picks `Const` over `BigInt`, and fuses a constant into an `IntImm`, when the literal is a small Int. So a program that names a literal from 2^47 up to 2^50 now runs fewer and different instructions, and no benchmark names one.

The other 1.2% came out of a loss. The first build changed only the tags and the range. map_churn gained its 23.2% and the geomean factor was 0.9899, but mandelbrot and list were 2.1% dearer, deriv 1.7%, cfold 0.9%, and ten rows in all were up with their VM counts unchanged. The disassembly showed two causes, and probes priced them. `Value::view` still tested the tags in the old order, and with the new numbers that compiled to a chain of 64-bit compares: Float, then small Int, then function, then cell, with a Bool last. A `JumpIfFalse`, a `Not` or a variant test paid 4 or 5 more. And `Value::float` written as 'finite, or else clamp' was compiled with no branch, so every Float add ran the clamp as well, and `float::op` computed both the sum and the difference and chose one: 1.5 to 3.5 more a Float instruction. The answer to the first is the layout's own shape. Every tagged word is a quiet NaN, the sign bit is a small Int, bit 50 is a cell or a constructor with no fields, bit 48 tells each pair apart, and no constructor makes a tag with bit 49 set. `view` tests those bits one at a time, which is one instruction each on ARM, and it is still exact on all 16 top bits. The answer to the second is a clamp that is out of line and cold, so `float` is one test of the answer. Probes, old binary against the final one, price each kind of instruction: an `Int` on two registers is 4 cheaper, an `IntImm` 1, a `Float` 6, a `FloatNeg` 8 and an `IntNeg` 7, and a `JumpUnlessCmpImm`, a `Const` and a `Call` with its `Ret` cost what they did. A turn of a list walk is 631 to 619, a Bool loop turn 513 to 505, and a turn that matches and compares an enum 929 to 910. `Machine::call` is 7,248 instructions long where it was 7,331. The rows follow the kinds they run: mandelbrot runs 242M Float instructions and lost 1,400M, nbody 72M and about 410M, and deriv and list run about 150M variant tests each and lost about 800M. So this 1.2% has causes that can be pointed at, unlike step 1's 1.7%, but it is only as safe as those two functions: step 8 rewrites the Float path and must keep the clamp out of it, and part of step 8's forecast for mandelbrot (the three compares of the old clamp) is already taken here.

One handler got dearer, and the gains hide it on every row, so the 1.2% is net of it. A tail call costs 2 more, and every loop turn of every benchmark is one. The bare loop turn of 332 to 329 is the sum: its `Int` is 4 cheaper, its `IntImm` 1 cheaper and its `TailCallSelf` 2 dearer. The cause is the layout itself. The old cell tag, shifted down, was -4, which ARM compares against in one instruction, and 0x7FFC is not a number ARM can compare against at all without a register. So `Machine::call_args`, which the three tail calls go through, loads the tag into a register before its loop, spills its return pointer to make room and reads it back at the end. The test it makes on an argument that is shared is one instruction shorter in exchange, but a tail call moves its arguments, so the 2 is what a loop pays, whatever the number of arguments. A `Call` goes through `enter_above` and pays nothing. From the board's counts that is 75M on list, 54M on mandelbrot, 51M on tak, 40M on spectral_norm and 29M on storage, which is as much as storage's whole net gain of 30M. The code is left as it is: the test is `as_cell`, written the plain way, and steps 10 and 14 take `call_args` away.

These unit costs are for later forecasts. The generator step `x = (x * 69069 + 1) % 2^32` costs 426 in a loop of its own where it cost 947, and 695 through a call where it cost 1,211. An Int result that needs a cell costs 427 more than one that does not: a loop turn whose sum is just past the range is 741, and the same turn inside it is 314. Past 2^50 nothing changed. The costs by kind are the ones above: `Int` 4 cheaper, `IntImm` 1, `IntNeg` 7, `Float` 6 (`Machine::float` is 48 instructions long where it was 54), `FloatNeg` 8, a tail call 2 dearer, and `JumpUnlessCmpImm`, `Const` and `Call` with `Ret` unchanged. With the last board's counts they give fib's row to 0.1M of its 89.7M, nbody's to 2M of 406M and mandelbrot's to 28M of 1,404M. Two paths got dearer that no benchmark runs. A Float answer that clamps costs 7 more than a finite one, where the two cost the same before, so it is 3 dearer than it was: a loop turn whose multiply overflows is 388 to 391, and the same turn with a finite answer 389 to 384. That is the price of the clamp being out of line. And a map lookup on a key that is a Bool, `Nil`, a function or a constructor with no fields costs 5 more in `hash::one` (32 to 37 by a trace of one lookup), which now builds the word it feeds where it fed the stored one, and 1 more in `map::get`. A lookup turn on a Bool key is 866 to 867 where the same turn on an Int key is 699 to 692, and on a `(Bool, Light)` tuple key 2,958 to 2,963. Steps 26 and 34 start from those.

One thing the design missed would have been seen by programs. hash.rs fed the stored word of `Nil`, a Bool, a function and a constructor with no fields into the hash, tag and all. Moving the tags moves those hashes, and with them the order of every map keyed by a Bool or an enum, or by a tuple or constructor that holds one. A test build that still hashes the word shows it: `map::keys` of five enum keys comes back `[Blue, Red, Green, Amber, White]` where it was `[Red, Green, White, Blue, Amber]`. So the hash now reads these values by what they are and feeds words of its own, and those words are the numbers the old tags were, so every hash is what it was and no map moved. An Int key was never at risk, because an Int hashes by its number whichever form it has. Two tests in hash.rs pin the hashes to what the old binary gave, and `a_maps_order_does_not_depend_on_how_its_keys_are_stored` in crates/scarlet_vm/tests/run.rs pins the orders end to end. Printing and `==` go through `view` and did not change, and this VM has no wire format yet to check. The changeset is `patch` and says only that the range is wider.

Three things are not built as the step's text reads. `Value::finite` is in value.rs and `Value::float` is built on it, but it is private: `float` is its only caller today, and the hawk gate refuses a `pub(crate)` that nothing outside its module uses. Step 8 opens it. The frozen-literal tag and the empty-array immediate are rows in value.rs's table and nothing else, with no constant and no `View`, because nothing makes or reads either yet, and `view` reads both as `Nil`. It reads every other word that no constructor makes as `Nil` too, the tags 0x7FFA, 0x7FFB and 0x7FFF and any immediate past `True`, and says nothing: `view` has no answer that means none of these, and the debug assert that once stopped a test build on such a word went with the rule against asserts. Nothing makes such a word today. `view_reads_every_word_as_the_table_says` pins which words they are, and whether `View` should have a variant for them that becomes a typed stop is the owner's to decide. The test of two operands at once (`a & b` at or above the Int tag) is pinned by a unit test and has no function, for the same reason. The cycles column that the core design asks for at this step was built in step 1.

The tests pin the following. In value.rs: every constructor's word is a NaN, for each of the 65,536 values the top 16 bits can take a word reads as anything but a Float only when it is a NaN, `view` reads each of them as the file's table says (the tags and immediates no constructor makes among them), `as_cell` and `small_int` agree with `view` on all of them, each kind has its own words, `finite` refuses the NaN of either chip and every tagged word, and the two words the file's header names read as `Nil` and the Int 0. In bigint.rs: divide and negate at both ends of the range, where `min / -1` and `-min` fit 64 bits and are still not small. In run.rs: `a_small_int_holds_51_bits_and_the_next_one_is_a_cell` and `divide_and_negate_are_right_at_both_ends_of_the_small_range` count cells at both edges, and `a_32_bit_generator_makes_no_cell` pins the forecast (10,000 steps made 10,518 cells on the old binary and make none now). The test programs and the example that crossed 2^47 to reach a heap Int now cross 2^50 (numerics, bitwise, hex_literals, enum_equality and examples/numbers.sca), and three golden files changed with them.

The binary, the boards, the probes, the first build, the test build and the toy files that show what the compiler makes of each tag test are in the perf scratch directory under `step5`, with `notes.txt` saying which is which. The three reviews' files and the probes and board that answer them are there too.

### Step 6. One arena: a cell is named by the index of its header word in one `Vec<u64>`, and the heap's methods keep their signatures. Long cells go behind a two-word stub.

From: heap 1. Needs: 1.

**Why here.** It is measured in a copy of the real VM, it changes no instruction and no handler, and the array, string and map kernels of later steps are all written against it.

**Expected gain.** Measured 0.915 on today's benchmarks. After step 3 the four array rows have less cell work left, so expect about 0.93. fib, tak and mandelbrot rose 1.4% to 2.1% in the port for an unknown reason; find out why before this lands.

**What happened.** The board moved a little more than forecast, and every row got cheaper. The geomean went from 4.65 to 4.26, a factor of 0.916 against the 0.93 expected and the 0.915 the design measured before step 3. richards runs 13.1% fewer machine instructions, queens 11.7%, towers 11.6%, bounce and list 10.8%, fannkuch and rbtree 10.7%, deriv and permute 10.4%, storage 10.3%, cfold 10.0%, closures 9.8%, sieve 9.4%, string_keys 8.9%, nbody 8.7%, binary_trees 8.3%, json 8.2%, strings 7.0% and map_churn 5.4%. The four rows that make almost no cells moved least: spectral_norm 2.0%, mandelbrot 1.6%, fib 0.7% and tak 0.5%. The VM's own counts are the same on all 23 rows and on `startup`: the same instructions kind for kind, and the same cells made, reused and live. The step changes how a cell is reached and nothing about which cells there are. Two earlier builds had boards of their own. The build before binaries got their own way to a long cell read 0.919, and the build the reviews read was 0.918. The reviews changed the free loop, the binary built-ins and how the arena grows, and each is described below.

fib, tak and mandelbrot did not rise, and the reason they rose in the design's port is found. The first build here made a bare loop turn cost 347 machine instructions where it cost 329, which is 5.5%. A trace of one turn, instruction by instruction in both builds, showed three causes, and none of them is in the heap. First, `Machine::call` kept the frame's `base` on the stack and read it back at each use. The heap's functions got smaller, so the optimizer put `int_of` inside `call` at its two uses, and with it there `base` lost its register. `int_of` is now marked to stay out of line, as it was before the step by the optimizer's own choice, and a turn is 4 cheaper for it (339 to 335). Second, the heap struct grew by 24 bytes and it sits first in `Machine`, so `passing` moved from offset 0x100 to 0x118. A call moves that Vec with one 16-byte load and one store, and each needs an instruction more when its offset is past 255 and not a multiple of 16: three a tail call. `Machine` now has `#[repr(C)]` with the loop's fields first and the heap last, so no later change to the heap's size can move them, and a turn is 3 cheaper again (335 to 332). A test in exec.rs pins where `passing` sits. Third, the way to a long cell's box is a call inside every reader of a cell, and the loop is 8 cheaper with that call marked cold (339 against 347), by where the registers land. A build with no long cells in its readers, which is what the design's port was, read 336, or 2.1% over, with `int_of` inside `call` and `passing` at the bad offset: that is the size of the rise the design saw and could not explain. With all three seen to, what is left is 3. A turn costs 332, because `call` now copies `base` to a second register before each dispatch, one `mov` an instruction, and I did not find a change that removes it without costing more elsewhere. The probes say what the rows that make no cells are made of. The `mov` is about 1 on every VM instruction: a turn of 4 instructions is 3 dearer and a Bool loop of 8.5 is 8 dearer. A turn with a `Call` and a `Ret` in it is level (582), and a turn of two Float adds and their constants is 2 cheaper (498 to 496). None of that is the arena's doing, in either direction, and it is why mandelbrot and spectral_norm gained more than fib and tak.

Probes give the unit costs, as the slope between two loop lengths, old binary against new. Reading a field of a record the loop keeps costs 101 where it cost 107. A list walk of two variant tests and two field reads a turn costs 567 where it cost 619. A record made, read twice and freed, with its cell coming back off the free list, costs 78 less a turn (1,245 to 1,167), a tuple the same way 102 less, and a closure made and called 120 less. Freeing one cell of a long list costs 106 where it cost 163. A share and a release of a cell that stays alive cost about 23 less the pair. A turn that makes two short strings and frees three (`'ab' + 'cd'`) costs 236 less (2,017 to 1,781). One thing got dearer: a cell taken at the arena's end, which is every cell a program makes before it first frees one of that size, costs 31 more than a cell taken at a chunk's end (579 to 610 a turn for a list that is built and kept). `carve` fills the new words with zeros through `Vec::resize`, which is a call to the system's `bzero`, and the maker then writes them again. The old chunk was zeroed once when it was made. Step 7's "pop, or append" writes the real words once and ends this.

The array write step 3 measured at about 390 and 620 is about 325 and 490 now. With the loop's own instructions beside it, a write in place to an array of 32 elements or fewer went from 463 to 402, at 5,000 elements from 730 to 606 and at 100,000 from 830 to 677. A copying write went from 2,091 to 1,476 at 16 elements and from 7,897 to 5,018 at 5,000, and a write that misses from 289 to 272. Pushes, slices and uncons moved the same way: a push at either end costs 137 less a turn, a slice 205 less, and a push onto a shared array 299 less. A `map::set` turn is 15 cheaper and a delete 38.

A cell of more than 254 words is the one case that got dearer. It keeps two words in the arena, a header whose size field is 0 and the number of a box, and its other words are in the box. 254 is the longest cell step 11's header can describe, so the line does not move again. A field read from a record of 300 fields costs 131 where a narrow one costs 101, and where it cost 107 when the record had a chunk of its own. That is 30 for the out-of-line way to the box, which is marked cold. json is where it shows. Its input is 25 KB, so it lives in a box, and the run reads it 28,473,004 times. With every reader on the cold path json gained 5.0% and not the 8.4% a build with no long cells gave it. So a binary's readers have their own door, `Heap::binary_data`, which is not cold. With it json gained 7.7%, and gains 8.2% now that the free loop is cheaper too. The same door on every reader costs every loop turn 4. strings makes 53,491 long cells and reads them 186,873 times, which is too few to show, so a string's readers still take the cold way. The step that gives text one door (25) should give it this one and measure.

Against step 5, and not only against a short cell, a long binary is dearer to read and a long cell is dearer to make. One read through `binary_data` costs 17 more than a read of a short binary and 7 to 14 more than the same read cost when the binary had a chunk of its own: on the build the reviews read, json's reads were 207M dearer than on a test build with no long cells, which is 7 each, and `binary::byte_size` on a 10 KB binary went from 517 to 531 a turn. A built-in that reads a binary a byte at a time looked its owner up for every byte, so `binary::append` onto 2,100 bytes came out 4.6% dearer than at step 5 (332,700 to 347,900 a turn) while the same call on 2,000 bytes was 6.9% cheaper. binary.rs now looks the owner up once for each run of reads, through `Opened`, and the append is about 14% cheaper than at step 5 on both sides of the line: 316,800 to 272,700 at 2,000 bytes, 332,700 to 286,900 at 2,100 and 630,600 to 543,200 at 4,000. A single read, like one `byte_size` or one node of json's tape, still pays the 7 to 14. Making a long cell costs about 450 to 620 more than at step 5: a turn that appends one byte to a string and takes its length went from 35,057 to 35,511 at 2,100 bytes and from 62,987 to 63,606 at 4,000, where the same turn at 1,000 bytes is 136 cheaper. The box is an allocation of its own, `long` fills it with zeros before the maker writes it, and the maker writes through the cold door. That is about 1% of such a turn today, because the turn also copies the whole string.

The heap's limit changed, and it was not what anyone thought. Before the step a chunk was 8/5 the size of the one before, and a chunk of more than 2^28 words was refused, so the heap had 30 chunks at most: 565,175,446 words, 4.21 GiB. Past that every new cell was `HeapFull`, whatever its size. A value bigger than the current chunk had a chunk of its own, which did not count, so a few very large binaries could pass 4.21 GiB and a list could not. Now the arena and the boxes together hold 2^31 words, 16 GiB, and one cell is still at most 2^28 words less one. Both ends are measured on real runs. A list of 150 million cells, 4.5 GiB, stops the old binary with "the program ran out of heap" and runs on the new one. On the new one 530 million cells run and 540 million stop. The changeset says so, and it is `major`, because a program that held more than 16 GiB in very large values ran before and stops now.

The arena never asks the system for room it may not use. `grow` doubles the room, as a `Vec` does, but stops at the limit, where the first build asked for up to twice the limit (20 GiB for a list of 4-word cells). When the system refuses a doubling, `grow` asks for half as much, then half of that, down to an eighth of the arena, and the heap is full when that is refused too. The first build fell back to exactly the words needed, which left no spare room, so every later cell would have moved the whole arena. The order of the requests is a function of its own (`growth`) with a test. A real refusal has none, because no test here controls the system's allocator: the three places a refusal becomes `HeapFull` (the arena's growth, a box, and the table of boxes) are read and not run. No test stops a whole program at the limit either, because only heap.rs's own tests can make a small heap.

Peak memory moved by under 3% on every row but two, deriv (9.6% less) and rbtree (8.4% less). I did not find out which of two things that is: a `Cell` is 4 bytes where it was 8, and the free lists are lists of them, and there is no unused tail of a chunk any more. The arena gives nothing back while the process lives, as the chunks gave nothing back. A long cell's box is freed with the cell, and that is the largest change in memory the step makes. The old heap kept every freed size on a free list of its own for the life of the process, so a string grown past 2 KB by appending left a dead cell behind at each length. Growing a string to 60 KB ten bytes at a time peaks at 186.7 MB on the step 5 binary and 5.9 MB now, and to 10 KB at 10.5 MB against 4.9 MB. Whether the process gets smaller when a box is freed is up to the system's allocator: on macOS a run that makes and drops five 512 MiB strings one at a time peaks at 3.77 GiB on both binaries.

Against the design's port, the string rows gain less and the scalar rows gain more, which is why the geomeans agree. As factors, string_keys is 0.911 where the port had 0.884, strings 0.930 against 0.912 and json 0.918 against 0.897, while fib, tak, mandelbrot and spectral_norm came in 1.4 to 3.2 points better than the port. Two causes are found. The first was this step's own. `release` fetched a dying cell's words and only then asked which of them hold values, so freeing a string cost 105 where the port's loop cost 80. It now works that out from the header first (`held_words`) and looks up no words for a cell that holds none. Freeing a string is about 17 cheaper for it (the `'ab' + 'cd'` turn went from 1,832 to 1,781), and string_keys went from 7.7% to 8.9% and strings from 6.4% to 7.0%. A test for the kinds that hold nothing, made ahead of everything else, got a string 7 cheaper still, but cost every constructor freed about 4 and the board 0.08%, so it was not kept. The second cause is not this step's. `Heap::string` is a call here, 79 a string as at step 5, and the optimizer put it inside `Machine::call` in the port. An `#[inline]` hint changes nothing. Step 7's 0.928 was measured on top of the port, so on these three rows it starts 1.8 to 2.7 points behind.

Some things are not as the design has them. The `outside` table is a `Vec` of boxes with a list of spare numbers, and a host handle is still an ordinary two-word cell: the design's `Outside` enum with handles in it belongs to the step that changes kinds. A box is put in the table before its stub is made, and the number that comes back is the one the stub holds; a stub that is refused gives the box back. The heap has a `limit` field, which is 2^31 in every heap a program gets and smaller in the tests that fill one. `held`, which says which of a cell's words are values, counts from the word after the header and is handed the cell's real length, so a long cell needs no case of its own in `hollow` and `copy`. Two lines of exec.rs changed, which the step said it would not touch: the `#[repr(C)]` and the `#[inline(never)]` above. binary.rs changed too, in how its built-ins reach a binary's words and not in what they do. A new cell is not appended after a `try_reserve` each time, as the design words it: `carve` tests the room first and only the cold `grow` asks the system, with word 0 kept back so that index 0 is never a cell.

The dead-cell detection of step 1 works as it did, and in two places it sees more. Freeing still zeroes the header, and a long cell's stub is zeroed the same way, so a second release of one stops where before it found a missing chunk. An index the arena does not have, 0 or anything past its end, reads as a freed cell: `release`, `unique`, `hollow`, `discard` and `copy` return the typed stop and nothing panics. What it cannot see is unchanged too: space handed out again hides a stale holder, and a share of a freed cell, or of word 0, still counts in it without complaint. Step 7 ends the second.

The tests in heap.rs replace the three chunk tests, and there are 46 of them. They pin that there is no arena until the first cell, that the arena is exactly as long as the cells carved from it and takes freed space again before it grows, that a cell keeps its name when the arena moves, and that 254 words is the longest cell in the arena. For long cells they pin a constructor of 300 fields through read, reuse in place and free, a tuple, a closure, a big Int, a copy, a splice in each direction between short and long, and a chain of a thousand long cells freed by one release. They pin the limit on a heap made small: the arena and the boxes are counted together, the arena's room never passes the limit, a refused cell leaves the heap as it was, and a long cell whose stub does not fit leaves no box behind and keeps its box number for the next one. Three tests in run.rs run a record of 300 fields, a constructor of 300 fields rebuilt in place a thousand times, and strings and binaries on both sides of the line end to end, with the audit, and print what the old binary prints.

The binary, the boards, the probes, the traces and the test builds are in the perf scratch directory under `step6`, with `notes.txt` saying which is which. The three reviews' files are there too, and the builds, probes and boards that answer them are under `step6/answer`.

### Step 7. The heap's fast paths: free lists threaded through the cells, a signed count, inline dup and drop, a free loop with no side Vec, and the audit. Three changes from the heap design: only big Int constants are frozen here, raw-cell rounding waits for step 27, and a count of 0 is a typed stop from the out-of-line branch.

From: heap 2. Needs: 6.

**Carried from step 6.** The arena, the stubs and the limit are built, on today's header and with today's `FreeLists`, `release` and `dying`. What this step starts from: a cell made off a free list, read twice and freed is 1,167 a turn in the `allocdrop` probe, freeing one cell of a chain is 106, and a cell taken at the arena's end costs 31 more than it did at step 5, because `carve` zero-fills it with `Vec::resize` (a call to `bzero`) before its maker writes it. Appending the real words is what ends that, so `alloc` has to take them. A long cell's box is written twice in the same way: `long` fills it with zeros and the maker writes it again, where the old chunk of its own came zeroed from the system. So the real words have to reach `long` too, as an `extend` after its `try_reserve_exact`. Making a long cell costs 450 to 620 more than at step 5 today. No cell in the arena is longer than 254 words, so `free` can be a table of 255 heads, and the `HashMap` half of `FreeLists` now only ever sees sizes 64 to 254: `alloc` sends a longer cell to `long` before it asks a free list. A long cell is a stub: its header's size field is 0 and its second word is its box's number, `free_cell` calls `unbox` for it, and `boxed` and `limit` count its words. The free loop has to do the same. Today's loop reads which words a dying cell holds from its header (`held_words`), so a string is freed without a look at its words. Keep that: with the words fetched first, freeing a string cost 105 where the design's port had 80, and it is about 88 now. The string rows still start behind the port this step's 0.928 was measured on: string_keys is 0.911 against the port's 0.884, strings 0.930 against 0.912 and json 0.918 against 0.897. **Expected gain.** Measured 0.928 on top of step 6; expect about 0.94 after step 3. Its frozen-literal gain (10.7% of string_keys) is booked to step 25.

**What happened.** The board moved further than forecast, and every row got cheaper. The geomean went from 4.267 to 3.819, a factor of 0.895 against the 0.94 expected and the 0.928 the design measured. Four boards gave 0.8949 to 0.8950. strings runs 16.6% fewer machine instructions, richards 16.1%, towers 15.8%, string_keys 15.5%, binary_trees 15.2%, queens 15.1%, rbtree 13.3%, deriv and json 12.7%, cfold 12.0%, bounce 11.9%, storage 11.8%, permute 10.5%, fannkuch 10.4%, list 9.4%, nbody 9.2%, closures 8.7% and sieve 7.4%. Cells made do not say which rows those are. list makes 62,000 cells and moved 9.4%, and map_churn makes 822,000 and moved 2.4%. The VM runs the same instructions kind for kind on all 23 rows, and reuses the same cells and ends with the same cells live. The factor is three things, and two of them are not the heap's. Every register access adds the register's number to the frame's base, and that add clamped where it now wraps. The sum is exact either way, because the base is no more than a `Vec`'s length, so nothing changes but one machine instruction an access. It is worth 0.981 on the board by itself (a build with the old add, against the step). It was made to pay for what comes next, and it is step 10's gain taken early: take it off step 10's forecast. A string's words are now built eight bytes at a time where they were built one, which is 6.8% of strings and 0.1% worse on string_keys, about 0.997. A loop that touches no cell is 1 to 6 a turn dearer without the wrapping add, and 2 to 5 cheaper with it.

The inline drop was built and measured, and it is not what landed. With share and release both inside `Machine::call`, a bare loop turn, which shares and releases nothing, went from 332 to 348. The loop's own values lose their registers: `base`, and in some builds `body` and `pc`, end up on the stack. About twenty builds priced the shapes, and the notes in the scratch directory list them. Putting a kernel or a handler out of line moved the bare turn by as much again in either direction, and no shape that puts the release inside was as good as the best without it. The step's shape is the cheapest found: the share is inside, the release is a call, and the call is marked cold, so the optimizer gives its registers to the paths that do not make it. Against the release inside, that is 12 a bare turn and 3% on the geomean of fifteen probes. With it a bare turn is 338, which is 6 over step 6, and the wrapping add brings it to 330. Step 9 replaces the loop and should try the inline release again.

A release that leaves the cell alive is 21, where the design has 13: the 8 are the call. The pair was 82 as an argument passed and dropped, and is 55, with the callee's `Drop` instruction in both. A record made off a free list, read twice and freed is 183 less a turn (1,167 to 984), a tuple of four 103 less, a closure made, called and freed 175 less. A count of 0 is a typed stop, from the out-of-line branch, as the plan settled it. `share` returns `DeadCell` for a freed cell, so step 1's hole is closed: a stale holder that shares, releases or asks `unique` stops at that instruction, and no share or release can write in a freed cell's header. A cell waiting to be freed is found too. The design ends the list of the dying with 0, which makes the last waiting cell's count the same number as a frozen one's. It ends with 2^31 - 1 here, the last index an arena can have, where no cell can be because a cell takes two words. Some things are not as the design has them. A freed cell's header is 0 and not its size, because every reader of a cell's words finds its size there, and 0 is what makes a freed cell read as empty. The size sits in the top half of the second word, over the next cell's index, where only the audit reads it. The check moved as the design has it. A Float instruction does its operator on the two words as they are and puts the answer through `Value::finite`, and a word that is no Float is a NaN and stops there. `Value::finite` and `Value::float` are still the only ways in from an f64, and `Value::unchecked_float` is the one way out that does not ask. Int operands are told by one `&`, and the operator runs on the 51 bits raised to the top of the word (`SmallInt`), so that the CPU's overflow flag is the range check. A constant is held in that form, made once by the loader.

Some things are not as the design has them. The stored comparisons have constant forms too (`IntEqK` to `IntGeK`, `FloatLtK` to `FloatGeK`), which the design leaves out. The old loader held a constant in a stored Int comparison, so without them json would run 596,100 more VM instructions and rbtree 300,000 more, and with them each fused jump is one stored comparison turned into a jump, for Floats as for Ints. That makes 55 instructions in place of 5, with `FloatFromInt` among them, and 115 kinds where there were 65. The fast path is not one function per operator but one with the operator as a constant (`int::fast`, `float::fast`), inlined where it is known, so a handler cannot pair one operator's fast path with another's slow one. The operator types are `Arith`, `Order` and `Compare` (`ops.rs`) in place of `IntOp` and `NumOp`, so an arithmetic slow path gives a value and a comparison gives a `bool`. The slow paths are methods of the machine, because they write a register, and the rules they follow are `int::op`, which is `i128` and then `num-bigint`, and `float::op`. `float::op` keeps its match on the operator, because the slow path and the generic `Num` need it, and no fast path reaches it. A `match` could not be written by a macro without putting all of it in one, so each handler is a line that names its instruction, its operator and a macro for its shape. The list that has every operator instruction beside its operator is in code.rs, as the patterns `int_arith!` and nine more. The loader finds the instruction for an operator in it (`instr_for!`), and the tests in exec/tests.rs and code/tests.rs take it from there too. A Float constant is held as a `Float`, a value that can be nothing else, as an Int one is held as a `SmallInt`. The test of a fused jump is a value (`Test`) that becomes an instruction only when it is given where to jump. The loader folds constants by the local and not by the instruction before: it loads each operator as it will be, and a constant whose every reader holds it gets no `Const`. `IntNeg`'s slow path is `int::neg`: `0 - x` in `i128` for an Int of one word and `num-bigint`'s negate for a longer one. An operand that is not an Int still stops with `NotBuiltYet` and not with `Broken`, which is what the code did and the design says otherwise. `FloatRem` needed a `PrimOp` of its own, because `%` on two Floats was lowered to the generic operator. The changeset is `patch`: nothing a program prints changes, and `scarlet dis --instrs` and `--counters` name new instructions.

The tests run every operator instruction in the list, the constant forms, the stored comparisons and the negates among them, each with the answer written over each of its operands too. The Int ones run on 32 edges paired with each other, the small range's two ends, one past each, one word, two words and 3 times 2^200, and on 200 numbers of every size up to 120 bits, against answers the tests work out with `num-bigint`. The Float ones run on both zeros, the smallest and the largest Float of each sign and sixteen between, against the rules written out in the test, bit for bit. An Int instruction whose answer has no room in the heap must stop with `HeapFull` and leave its operands. Every instruction is handed every kind of word in each operand and must stop with the word's name and leave no cell. A program in tests/run.rs does each operator with constants on both sides, stored and branched on, next to the same operator on any number, which is another instruction, at the end of each range, and fails if any operator instruction there is never ran. The same runs on fewer values under `--audit=each`. Seventeen wrong handlers, rules, rows of the list and loader choices, put in by hand, were each caught. 4,900 random programs of operators printed the same on the old binary and the new one, 1.0 million lines, under `--audit` (3,400 of them on the build before the last clean-ups), and 60 more under `--audit=each`. 900 more under `--audit` and 20 under `--audit=each` did on the final build.

Startup is level: 36.3M to 36.4M, which is inside its spread. The loader does more for each operator, because it loads one that has a constant beside it twice to learn whether the constant is held, so a program that is one function of 400 to 1,600 operators costs 1.7% to 1.8% more to load and run once. The binary is 50 KB larger. `Machine::call` is 7,089 machine instructions long where it was 7,095, with two indirect branches where it had six.

### Step 9. One 16-byte `Ins<R>` enum in one code array, matched in place, with `Ins::operands` as the single list of each instruction's registers and roles (including several written registers and writes on the hit edge only), cold state behind one `&mut Rest`, and the loader split into translate, live, alloc and emit.

From: core 3. Needs: 8.

**Carried from step 2.** The emit pass leaves out a `Move` whose two ends got the same register. It does nothing, and it is most of the Moves that sieve, fannkuch, permute, queens, map_churn and string_keys run.

**Carried from step 6.** A bare loop turn is 332, where it was 329 after step 5. The 3 is `Machine::call` copying `base` to a second register before each dispatch, which came with step 6 and has no cause in the source. Two things in exec.rs hold the rest of that loss off, and both go when this step and step 10 replace the loop: `int_of` is `#[inline(never)]`, because inside `call` it cost `base` its register (4 a turn), and `Machine` is `#[repr(C)]` with the loop's fields first and the heap last, because a Vec the loop loads 16 bytes at a time must not sit at an offset past 255 that is not a multiple of 16 (3 a call). `Rest` should keep the heap behind the loop's own fields for the same reason. Step 6's traces of one loop turn, old build against new, are in the perf scratch directory under `step6/trace`.

**Carried from step 7.** The release is a call, marked cold (`Heap::release`), and the share is inline. Inside today's loop an inline release cost a bare turn 12, because `base` and `body` lost their registers to the handlers. With handlers that reach cold state through `&mut Rest`, try it inside again: it is worth 8 a release of a cell that stays alive. `Instr::Audit` is new, with no operands, and it is in every load made for `--audit=each`. `base` still sits on the stack in `Machine::call`, read back at each use, and the dispatch no longer copies it. Results that come back in memory cost too: each handler that returns a `Result<Value, Stop>` has a place on the stack for it, and in some of step 7's builds the compiler kept the address of such a place in a register across the loop. A `Stop` behind a `Box` comes back in two registers. Step 7's builds and traces are under `step7/notes.txt` and `step7/trace` in the perf scratch directory.

**Carried from step 8.** A bare loop turn is 297 and dispatch is 13, the same instructions as before step 8. The unit costs in step 8's 'What happened' are today's. The operator instructions are listed by family in code.rs (`int_arith!` and the rest), and the six places that list instructions name the families. `Ins::operands` replaces those places, and the loader and the tests still need a list that has each instruction beside its operator and each comparison beside its jump. The design's enum has no constant forms of the stored comparisons. Step 8 has ten, and json and rbtree run them. A constant is held as a `SmallInt` or a `Float`, 8 bytes each. Each slow path writes its register itself, which kept the fast path from joining anything and was worth 0.0025. The fused jumps share one block that loads the target, so a jump that is taken is 1 dearer than one that is not. Four kernels are out of line to keep the loop's registers: `array::concat`, `array::slice`, `map::set_owned` and `map::delete_owned`. The `#[inline(never)]` on `int_of` that step 6 needed is gone, because nothing in the loop calls it now (`int::read`). A boxed `Stop` was tried on the slow paths and gained nothing, because nothing on the fast path goes near it. `Loader::held_numbers` decides which constants get no register by loading each operator once in advance, and `expr` then loads it again. The two agree only because both call `prim` on tables that do not change. A fusion added to `expr` alone would leave an instruction reading a register nothing wrote, and nothing checks for that. When the loader is split, have the pass that decides be the one that makes the instruction, which is also the 1.8% a long function pays.

**Why here.** This is the instruction format. Doing it now means every instruction the other four areas add is written once, in the final enum.

**Expected gain.** About 0.955: dispatch falls from 15 to 9 on every VM instruction. Accept it when objdump shows a 9-instruction dispatch block and one indirect branch.

**What happened.** The board moved almost four times as far as forecast, and every row got cheaper. The geomean went from 3.59 to 2.98, a factor of 0.831 against the 0.955 expected. fib runs 28.3% fewer machine instructions, tak 28.1%, towers and mandelbrot 21.7%, list 20.0%, spectral_norm 19.9%, binary_trees and cfold 19.4%, queens and json 17.5%, deriv and permute 17.4%, rbtree 16.2%, storage 16.1%, closures 15.7%, sieve 15.0%, map_churn 14.2%, fannkuch 13.0%, nbody 12.9%, richards 11.6%, string_keys 8.7%, bounce 8.5% and strings 4.4%. objdump shows what the step asks for: the dispatch block of `Machine::steps` is 9 machine instructions (`cmp; b.hs; add; add; ldrb; adr; ldrh; add; br`) where it was 13, and the function has one indirect branch where it had two. An arm that ends in a call puts back the code's address and the jump table's, which is 3 more on its way there. A bare loop turn is 201 where it was 297.

Dispatch by itself, 4 machine instructions on each of 5.4 billion, is about a quarter of the gain. The design asked for two things that are not built, and a third is built that it did not ask for. `fail`, which stores where a stopped run was, and the `At` it stores are left to step 10: nothing reads them until a call asks for more stack, and the compiler refuses a field nothing reads. Operand lists do not name constants. The design offered that to keep a long literal inside a frame, and making the literal in pieces does it without a test on every operand of every list. The pieces are made only for a function that does not fit, by a rewrite of its Core IR (`code/literals.rs`), so every function that fits makes the cells it always made. A piece is put on an array's end as one leaf (`array::extend_owned`), and a literal with a spread is made in pieces too. A literal past the limit that is made again and again costs more than it did: a turn that makes one of 260 distinct constants is 20,656 to 28,316, of 400 is 30,061 to 43,572 and of 1,000 is 70,916 to 111,746, and 1,000 elements make 63 cells where they made 34. The plan's decision 5 says more than 250 is a compile error, and no step had it, so it is here (`CheckError::TooManyParameters` and `TooManyArguments`, and `MAX_PARAMS` in scarlet_ir). It refuses a function, a lambda, a function type and a constructor read as a function with more, and a call with more.

Other things differ from the text too. The list of an instruction's registers is not a function that names each instruction again. Each field's type says its role, and the macro that declares the enum writes `operands`, the flags pass, the jump target, the kinds, the placing and the listing from it, so an instruction is added in two places: the declaration and the loop. The design's kill bits, `dups` masks and call forms by whether an argument is shared are for steps 10 and 39 to 41, and the single-register `Drop` is as it was. The type parameter carries the rest of what differs between the loader's enum and the loop's: what a jump, a call and a frame's size are named by. A constructor's type and variant sit in the instruction in six bytes and not in a side table. `Frame` does not hold an entry's place, because nothing needs the running function: a call by name and a return hold their frame sizes, a tail call to self holds where it goes, and the two stops that name the function find it from the place. Parameters are pinned to the first registers, which step 10 was to do, so an entry has no list of them.

The loader is five files, each with tests of its own. Liveness goes over a function once, from the end, with one set that grows and shrinks, because Core IR's jumps all go forward. It was a bit per value per instruction, run twice to a fixed point. A colouring took a bit matrix of values by values and takes the clashes as pairs. A cell that Perceus keeps for a constructor of the same turn is a value like any other. A comparison and the `if` on it become one jump over any constants between them. A value read on a path that did not write it is found by the same walk, at no cost, since it is held where the function starts, and the function then stops when it is called. A function whose last instruction may go on past it is refused in the same place, and `emit` refuses an instruction that names a list its function does not have. Startup is level (36.34M to 36.28M, inside its spread). A function of 400 operators loads and runs for 39% less than it did, of 1,600 for 76% less and of 6,400 for 91% less, a function of 1,000 strings and sums for 69% less and of 4,000 for 90% less, a match of 400 arms for 23% less, 1,500 small functions for 12% less and a module of 1,000 constants for 62% less. Four times the length costs 4.4 times the load at the top of that range, where it cost 12.5 times before. With 0 the same compare refuses them, a string, a stub and a freed cell, and the free loop loses nothing, because a scan of 0 had to be looked at anyway to tell a handle and a stub. A shape's top half is not in an instruction: a constructor there is `Ctor`, its class and its variant, 4 bytes, and the header is worked out from the class and the number of fields, so the two can never differ. Step 13's compare is built from the same two. `Element` is gone, and a tuple's element is read by `Field`. A freed cell's header is still 0 with its size in the next word, because every reader of a cell's words finds their number in the header. The classes 1 to 14 are in an order of their own, so that the four kinds that hold nothing are told by one compare. Nothing outside a load looks a constructor up by its type: the stdlib's that the VM builds (`Abi`) are renamed once, as the program loads, to what the VM makes them with. `Abi` in scarlet_ir says how many fields each has (`Fields<3>`), and the loader refuses one that is not as it says. So `None` is a value word in the loaded program and `Some` a `CtorOf<1>`, which can only be built with one value: a wrong number in the VM's own code does not compile, and a wrong number in a program's types does not load. Neither `Field` nor `GetCapture` asks what kind of cell it reads, where `Field`, `Element` and `GetCapture` each did. They ask whether the index is below the scan, so a wrong `Field` of an array's leaf, or of the first word of a binary slice, would give a value where it used to stop the run. Only a compiler mistake can make one. `hollow` keeps only a constructor's cell. `variant(cell)` stays, as the name the table gives for a cell's class, because printing and the hash need it.

Carrying the variant in the reference is where a wrong answer could have come from, so each place that holds one was gone through. `Cell` is the index and the variant together, and only the heap makes one, so a copy, a compare or a store cannot lose the variant. Inside the heap a cell is an `Index` alone wherever it must not be named (free lists, the list of the dying, a count, an audit's walk), and a `Value` cannot be made from one. A cell with no variant of its own has 0. A constructor written over a hollowed one gives back a new name, freed space is named by what it becomes, and a copy is named as what it copies. A constant's cell is kept by its whole name. `==` compares words first, which two references to one cell always pass, and then classes. The hash feeds the type and the variant the table gives, and no map moved: the hashes of ten constructors, a closure and a tuple are pinned to what the old binary gave. The loader's literals hold no constructor cell.

The limits. A program may have 65,520 constructors with fields among the types it names, the prelude's and the stdlib modules' with them, and one past that does not load and stops with the new `Stop::TooManyConstructors`. The old binary ran such a program, and no program in the repository comes near it. A type may have 65,536 variants, which the compiler already refused to pass, and all of them may have no fields. A record of 253 fields is the widest in the arena, where 252 was, and a wider one has a box, as a closure of more than 252 captures does. A constructor of 1,000 fields that is made from a few values loads and runs, because only values count against a function's 254.

The audit knows the header. It checks that each class is one a cell can have, that the scan and the size go with it, that a constructor's cell has as many fields as the table says, that a closure starts with a function, that a stub's box is there and too long for the arena, that a freed cell's header says nothing, and that every reference, a root's, a cell's or a constant's, carries the variant of the class it leads to. Four faults are new or have grown: `Misshapen`, `WrongVariant`, `NoFunction` and what `NoCell` refuses. Each is made by hand in heap/audit/tests.rs, over a cell of every kind and a long one of each kind that can be long, and seen to be found. The machine's tests read a field at eleven indexes and a capture at twelve from a cell of every kind, some of them long, and a field from every other kind of value, and pin which give a value and which stop with a typed error. Showing a value no longer makes up a name for a constructor the program does not list: it stops with `NoCtor`, as the loader does. `variants.sca` is a new golden program, which the old binary prints the same, and it runs under `--audit=each` with the others. Tests in run.rs match, rebuild and number every variant of types of 2 to 300, and take records and constructors of 252 to 1,000 fields through reading, comparing, hashing, showing, matching and rebuilding in place.

1,900 random programs of constructors printed the same 3.8 million lines on the old binary and the new one under `--audit`, with the same VM instructions and the same cells made, reused and live, and 154 printed the same under `--audit=each`. They have types of 1 to 60 variants, generic ones, types that spell variants alike, records of up to 320 fields, matches that rebuild as the same variant and as others, updates, closures of up to 30 captures, maps keyed by constructors and by tuples, and recursions of up to 300 levels. 400 programs of step 9's kind and 293 of step 10's printed the same too. 91 wrong changes, to the name a cell is given, the header, the scans, the free loop, the field read, the table of constructors, the tags, the audit and every reader of a class, were put in one at a time. 90 were caught, five of them only after a test was added. The other sends a scan of 1 to the kind to be asked, which gives the same answer.

Read by itself the step is worth its factor of 0.9303. The header, the variant in the word and the table are 0.948 of that, and the rest is 0.982 of shaping the loop round them, which later steps cannot have again: about 0.994 is step 14's, and about 0.996 is the width of an error and the constructor's two ways. What later steps take from it: step 12 the 4-byte `Ctor` and the maker's two ways, step 13 the header's top half as one number, step 16 the same compare, step 18 the variant in the word, step 22 the scan over values that come first, and step 25 the header for a frozen literal.

### Step 12. Constructors, tuples and closures filled straight from the registers: `Con1` to `Con3` (and up to seven fields, which fit) with the shape inline, and a list form for wider cells.

From: heap 4. Needs: 11.

**Carried from step 7.** `Heap::cell` is the one maker. It takes the words before a constructor's fields as an array and the rest as an iterator, pops inline and appends out of line, so the fixed forms can hand it an array of operands. Popping a cell and writing its header and first word is about 18 machine instructions, and 3 of them count it in `made`. An append of a constructor of two fields is 73, with a prologue that saves every register, where the design's probe had 64 for one word less.

**Carried from step 9.** `Ctor` holds six fields in the instruction and `CtorOver` five, and a wider one takes a list (`CtorWide`). richards makes 3.6 million of its 10.1 million constructors through the list and nbody all of its 2.5 million, because they have seven. **Carried from step 10.** What fills a cell are functions of their own in exec.rs now (`make`, `make_wide`, `shared`, `pass_few`), which take the window and `Rest`, and `passing` is a field of `Rest`. A handler that shares pays for the share even when it has none to make: a call that takes all its arguments is 9 machine instructions cheaper without it in the arm. So give a constructor that takes all its fields a form of its own, chosen as a call's is (`Ins::taking`), and measure that first.

**Carried from step 11.** A constructor sits in an instruction as `Ctor`, its class and its variant, which is 4 bytes where it was 6. So `Ctor` has room for eight fields beside it and `CtorOver` for seven, where they hold six and five today, and richards' seven fit. A header is worked out from the class and the number of fields, so a form with a fixed number of them has all but the class as a constant, and nothing needs the header's top half in the instruction. The handler already takes a freed cell in a straight line (`Heap::ctor_in_freed`) and goes to `Heap::ctor_at_end` when there is none, and the two do not join: joined, they cost each constructor about 4. The fields still go through the small array on the Rust stack, and a tuple's and a closure's through a `Vec`, so what this step takes away is all still there. One function in exec.rs makes every constructor, tuple and closure (`new_cell`), whatever kind of operands it is given (`Hands`), and the Vec for tuples and closures is gone with `make`, `make_wide` and `shared`. The constructor over a kept cell (`over`) is made of the same parts: it writes the values straight into the cell that was kept, and when none fits it goes on as `new_cell` does. So `pass_few`, its array and `passing` are left for the calls alone. The order is not the design's. The design's fixed forms read every operand, moved or shared, and then take the cell, and its list form takes the cell and fills it as it shares. Either leaves something half done when the cell is refused. Here the shares come first, in order. Then the space of a freed cell is taken, or the cell is added by a call, and only then do the values leave their registers, straight into it. So a refused cell has taken nothing. What was added is taken back, every register holds what it did, and every count is right, where the old code lost the fields it had gathered. There are three exceptions. A constructor over a kept cell that does not fit has given that cell up first, because the new one may go in its space. A share that takes a count to its end leaves it never counted, and that is not undone. And a freed cell among the operands, which only a bug in the VM can put there, stops the instruction with the references before it still added. The heap's half is that it asks for a long cell's box and stub before it asks for a value. The shape is not a word in the instruction: it is the 4-byte `Ctor`, as at step 11, and the header and the name are worked out from it at 5 machine instructions, with the number of fields a constant in the fixed forms. What a cell starts as is a `Shape` in heap.rs, and a closure's has its function in front.

### Step 13. `Open` (heap's Unpack): one instruction that takes a dying cell apart, found by the loader on a match arm that starts with the scrutinee's drop. Reuse tokens, DropReuse, `hollow`, `fits_ctor` and `Body::carried` are deleted.

From: heap 5. Needs: 12.

**Carried from step 9.** What goes with the tokens: `CtorOver`, `CtorOverWide`, `DropReuse`, `TailCallSelfKeep`, `KeepList`, `Role::Keep`, the `Kept` and `Over` fields, `Translator::kept`, `Draft::carried`, `Survey::reused` and `carried`, the registers of their own in `alloc::allocate`, and `Machine::keep`, `over`, `again` and `passing`'s use in them. `Open` writes several registers: a field list of `Dst`s is enough, and `live::clash` already makes a clash between every two registers one instruction writes, which `values_written_together_clash` tests on operands made by hand because no instruction has two yet.

**Carried from step 10.** `Machine::keep` is `Window::keep`, `over` is a function in exec.rs, and `again` is `rewrite_keeping` there and in frame.rs.

**Carried from step 11.** `Open` compares `header >> 32` with one number, as the design has it. A long cell's is the class alone, so its test cannot pass for a narrow one's, and the shape test on a stub passes only for a stub: do not read its second word as a field. How `open` goes. `Heap::open` compares the whole header with the one that a lone cell of that constructor has. When they are equal nothing else holds the cell: the free list takes it, in two stores and no call, and its fields are the caller's. When only the top halves are equal and the count is above 1, the count goes down by one and the fields are only read. Any other cell, a long one, one whose count is at its end, one that is never counted, a freed one or the wrong one, is left to `open_apart`, which is cold and takes a cell of any length. Then the fields go to their registers, and what each register held, like each field that has none, is released by a call to `release_apart` when it is a cell. Those calls were the difference between a factor of 0.956 and 0.946: inlined, the release made every arm of the loop dearer. The other difference was the shared path. As a call with the fields in a vector it made list 70% dearer than step 12, because a list that something else holds is all of list's work. Inside the loop for one field and two, and as a call for three and up, list is 22% cheaper.

The order of `open` is not the design's, and the design's is wrong in two ways. It empties the source and gives the cell up first, and then writes each register like any other, so a value that the register held is released in the middle. First, a stop in the middle, at a freed cell, loses the fields not yet written. Here every field is written and only then is the stop given. Second, and this one can go wrong in a correct program: when something else holds the cell, that something may be one of the registers the fields go to. Releasing the value there can free the cell while the rest of its fields are still to be shared. So a held cell has all the references of its fields added first, then the source is emptied, and only then is anything released. `open_apart`, which goes field by field, keeps its own reference to the end for the same reason. So at a freed field of a held cell, where `open` has changed nothing, it has written the fields before that one, and it puts the cell back in its register. Tests with the cell in the first register and in the last (`Else::FirstDst`, `Else::LastDst`) fail on the one-pass order. That safety costs 0.6% of the board: a one-pass build was 0.936 against 0.943.

**Expected gain.** Core measured it as the difference of two probe builds: fib about 165 to 112 a call, tak about 193 to 104, a geomean factor of 0.90 to 0.91 on core's table. With calls already removed by inlining, expect about 0.92.

**Carried from step 10.** fib is 137 a call with the sweeps kept, not 165, and tak 155, not 193, so there are 25 and 51 left for this step on them. What it takes away: 4 machine instructions on every register written (the old value read, its test and a branch), 2 on every argument taken (the `Nil`), and in a `Ret` 6 to 8 for each register of the frame and 3 to start. Half a sweep is gone already, since only a cell is written over. Read step 40's note on register 1 first: a call by name leaves it unwritten, and only the sweep makes sure that what is there is no cell.

**Carried from step 14.** The design's `Clear {from, n}` was never built: there are `Drop`s of the registers that may hold a cell, or one `DropWide` of a list of them when there are more than two, and no sweep at a tail call, but for `TailCallValue`, which sweeps the frame in `Window::rewrite`. A tail call costs its moves, its drops and one instruction.

### Step 42. `Open` placed by the rc pass wherever an owned local of proved shape is only read in parts and then dies, not just at an arm's head. Decide from the scoreboard after step 41.

### Step 43. Borrowed parameters by Lean's inference with rules O1 to O5, a `Hold` on binds, and an owned twin for closures and for tail calls that hand over something the frame owns. Decide from the scoreboard.

### Step 44. An update that takes a field, done in rc. Most of it is already in the lowering rule of steps 4 and 16, so this step is to check that rule against rc's wider view and delete whichever is redundant.

### Step 45. `Take` and reuse specialisation of ordinary rebuilds, with the loader's hole check. Gated: build it only if the scoreboard after step 44 still shows rebuilds that keep most of their fields.

From: heap 6 (the Take half). Needs: 44.

**Why here.** It is the cheapest form on the toy (157 against 311), but a hole is a Nil that nothing at run time can tell from a real one, and compiler's model saw permute get worse when every rebuild became an update.

**Expected gain.** About 0.995 at most (rbtree's `ins` arms, cfold under 1%).

**Carried from step 13.** Rebuilds start lower than this step's design has them. The fields that stay where they are save one load and one store each of that.

### Step 46. Grow a string or binary in place by reallocating to a power-of-two size, with a new `string_build` benchmark and its twins to show it.

From: text 12. Needs: 30.

**Carried from step 6.** A string or binary past 2 KB already has an allocation of its own, the box of its long cell, and the box is freed with the cell. So growing one in place can be a `realloc` of that box, and the stub in the arena does not move. The arena cannot give room back, which is why step 6 keeps long cells out of it: a string grown to 60 KB ten bytes at a time peaked at 186.7 MB when every freed length stayed on a free list, and peaks at 5.9 MB now.

**Why here.** Nothing on today's scoreboard shows it working or failing, and `string::join` already exists as the linear path, so it waits for its own benchmark.

**Expected gain.** 0.9996 on today's scoreboard. Its real effect is that a loop that builds a string by appending becomes linear.

### Step 48. Functions wider than the window: a spill stack with `Spill` and `Unspill`, which the verifier treats as more registers.

From: core 7. Needs: 41.

**Carried from step 9.** A function that does not fit is `Unfit::TooWide` in code.rs, which `live` finds when more than 254 values are held at once and `alloc` when the colouring needs more. It is tried once more with its long literals in pieces (code/literals.rs) and then becomes one `Bad`. Both go when values can be spilled, unless pieces are still wanted for what they save in registers. The note about 133 registers no longer holds for the tests: two use all 254.

**Carried from step 10.** The limit is `MAX_VALUES` in frame.rs, 254, from a window of 256 less the link and the closure. The link has room for a frame of 65,535 registers, but a callee's window starts where the frame ends, so a frame wider than a window would need its spilled values somewhere else, as the design has it.

**Why here.** No program in the repository needs it, so it is built once, after the ownership rules it must follow have stopped changing. Move it up if a real program stops for it.

**Expected gain.** None for speed. It removes a stop.

**Carried from step 14.** The moves of a tail call are between registers and need none to spare, and a callee of more parameters than this frame has registers writes above it. A parameter that spills would be a move to the spill stack in the same set.

### Step 49. Four optional pieces, each on its own measurement: several results in registers, reuse tokens, borrows rooted in a local, and kept-field masks.

From: compiler 14. Needs: 43.

**Why here.** Each adds a state to a checker or changes the return path, and each has a small measured ceiling.

### Step 50. Count reductions at calls and at a `Loop` instruction, with `Pause::OutOfFuel` through the same door as `NeedStack`. This belongs to the processes work, not the speed work.

From: core 8. Needs: 41.

**Why here.** The owner has put processes on hold. The earlier steps keep it possible: a call that cannot go ahead has written nothing and runs again.

**Carried from step 10.** The door is built: `steps` returns a `Pause`, `Done` or `NeedStack`, and the machine's loop around it starts it again at an `At`. A variant that carries one more value that the arm does not already have in a register cost 2.7% of the board, through registers the loop then lost, where one that carries a field of the instruction cost nothing. Measure `OutOfFuel` for that. A process's stack starts at 272 registers, 2.1 KB, and a call from its first frame makes it longer by what it needs and a sixteenth. `Stop::StackFull` should become the process's own death.

**Expected gain.** A cost: fib 112 to about 116 to 125 a call, the bare loop 87 to about 91 to 94.

**Carried from step 14.** A tail call to a known function ends in one of two instructions, a `Jump` back to the function's start and an `Enter`, so those are the `Loop` this step counts at. Counting at a `Jump` would count every jump, so a jump back needs a kind of its own.
